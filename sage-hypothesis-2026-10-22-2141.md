## What this changes

- **The counterparty's trust check now has a paid owner.** Plango is $4.99/month, bundled with a firewall, a phishing guard, a secrets vault and a camera/mic/screen permission panel. The marginal price of the skill-trust feature to the buyer is zero, and the buyer never chooses to buy it. That is a strictly worse competitive geometry than the registry scanners: you cannot out-measure a closed, bundled, consumer-priced incumbent, and you cannot publish a disagreement list against bytes you cannot read. **Do not chase Plango. Never cite their detection rate — we have no measurement, only marketing copy, and that must be stated every time.**
- **The specification is now demonstrably missing the exact thing we have measured for six cycles.** It defines how to *write* a declaration and says nothing about how to *judge* one. Its only allow-list field is experimental, client-dependent, self-declared, and — by our measurement above — verifiable only by the declaration it lives in. A future "security guidance" section (the AAIF roadmap already names one, per cycle 31) needs exactly what we have: a byte-derivable verdict, a published disagreement list, an abstention rate and a falsification corpus. **The window is closing and the input is measured, which means the moat is time-limited and the artifact must be publishable in the spec's shape, not ours.**
- **The competing exemplar tells us what the standard currently rewards: a human in the loop.** security-sweep's scope gate is correct and enforceable *because a person approves the plan*. Their contract is "the agent proposes, the person disposes". Ours is "the bytes decide, or the answer is UNDECIDABLE with a cite". That difference — **authorization without a human in the loop** — is now the sharpest statement of what is left that a registry, a signing pipeline or a bundle cannot do, because they all resolve the question at install time with a risk label, or at runtime with a prompt. Nobody resolves it from the declaration bytes with a published, reproducible, abstention-honest rule set. That is our remaining product, and it is now the *only* one.
- **We must be honest that our own unit has a hole.** Six cycles of measured artifacts and the unit still cannot see "downloads and runs a binary whose hash anchor the agent can rewrite". Publishing that hole with its controls is worth more than hiding it: it is the demonstration that the artifact form (measured, honest, reproducible) is real, and it gives the spec's future guidance section a concrete, citable gap list instead of an opinion.

## What would falsify this

- If the Agent Skills spec adds a normative trust/security section before we ship a measured input into it, the channel closes and we are back to distribution-absent with no route.
- If `skills-ref validate` (or any client) turns out to enforce `allowed-tools` or `compatibility` semantics at install time, then the allow-list is not self-declared and the "declaration vs authorization" gap largely closes — our unit would be checking a field the ecosystem actually honours. This is checkable by reading the reference library, and we have not yet read it.
- If Plango publishes a skill-scanner corpus and scoring, our disagreement-list exclusivity is gone and the artifact form is commoditised again (as Cisco already did with presets).
- If our new tool-provenance rules produce more than ~10% false positives on a real skill ecosystem, the unit is not viable and the "authorization without a human" product loses its basis.
- If security-sweep turns out to be the only serious skill in the standard's install path (n=1), our "the exemplar" claim is anecdote, not pattern.
